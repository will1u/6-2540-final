# Bond pad size matters less than spacing

> **Superseded in part (2026-11-11).** The pad pitch is now 360 µm, not 180 µm (lines 7, 27 and 68; team adoption still pending). The v4 stack has no 10 µm SiO₂ (D2), so the 10 µm SiO₂ / 6 µm Au stack (line 59), the enlarged D2 openings (recommendation 3) and the T2 Au / D2 / M1 test (recommendation 5) no longer apply. Current: [die layout](../../fab/LAYOUT.md) and [layer stack](../../fab/STACK.md).

Our 120 × 200 µm Au pads at 180 µm pitch are already about twice the size every verified wire-bonding rule asks for. The university standard is 100 × 100 µm with a 60 µm floor, and the foundry minimum for 25 µm Au ball bonds is a 55 µm opening on a 65 µm pitch. They also sit inside the range used by published surface traps: Sandia's 95 µm pads on a 100 µm pitch, and an older MIT trap's 300 × 300 µm Al pads. Electrically, pad area hardly matters on this stack. The RF pad is about 0.17 pF, roughly 5 % of the die's ≥ 3.4 pF of on-chip RF capacitance and under 1 % of a typical ~20 pF resonator load. On the DC electrodes and the NbN SNSPD the leads dominate by 3× and 40× respectively [team calc]. For optical access, what the literature cares about is where the bond wires sit and how high they loop, not how big the pads are. Three things matter more than pad size for this die. First, the Au ground fill comes within **10 µm** of every pad (measured on `trap_die_v3_A30.gds` [team calc]), far tighter than the 60 µm pad spacing facilities treat as a minimum. Second, every Au pad sits over a near-continuous Al plane, the same pad-over-insulator-over-ground geometry behind the one documented trap failure caused by bond pads. Third, the SNSPD pads are the hardest bonds on the die: thin Ti/Au at the bottom of 10 µm wells only 90 µm wide. The changes worth making are a wider fill keep-out around pads, larger SNSPD openings, wedge bonding on the SNSPD, and bond-and-leakage tests on the real stack before any trap die is bonded.

## Facility rules put the floor near 60 µm, and our pads clear it twice over

A 25 µm (1 mil) Au ball bond is about 75 µm across. UC Davis's ball-bonder page says "ideally bond pads should be 100x100um square or larger" ([UC Davis CNM2](https://cnm2.ucdavis.edu/equipment/west-bond-7700e-ball-bonder), excerpt only). Free-air balls are generally 2-3 wire diameters ([ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/docview/2474760246), excerpt only). Wedge feet are narrower but longer. An ESCC visual-inspection specification rejects feet narrower than 1.2 or wider than 3 wire diameters, and shorter than 1.5 or longer than 5 ([escies.org](https://escies.org/download/specdraftapppub?id=2884), excerpt only). For 25 µm wire that gives an acceptable foot of **about 30-75 µm wide by 38-125 µm long** [team calc]. The best-documented facility rule is EPFL CMi's for its manual TPT HB10: **standard pads 100 × 100 µm with 100 µm spacing, reducible to 60 µm "but not more"**. Pads go in a single line no more than 500 µm from the die edge, the package pads should be at least 300 × 500 µm, and the die-to-package gap should be 0.5-2 mm ([EPFL CMi HB10 guidelines](https://www.epfl.ch/research/facilities/cmi/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/HB10_guidelines.pdf)). Europractice's machine-bonding service is tighter. For 25 µm Au ball bonds it asks for an **opening > 55 × 55 µm, pitch > 65 µm, wire ≤ 6 mm and wire angle < 45°**, with at most two wires per pad ([Europractice 2024](https://europractice-ic.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/ASIC_Prototype_Packaging_Design_Rules_2024.pdf)). CERN's bond lab adds the two qualitative rules that matter most for hand bonding. Al wedge bonds want a rectangular pad and Au ball bonds a square one, and it is "prudent to make the bond pad as large as is realistically possible" ([CERN Bond Lab](https://bondlab.web.cern.ch/Recommendations.html)).

Measured against these rules, the signal pads are generous. A 120 µm-wide pad leaves **±22 µm of placement margin** around a 75 µm ball or a maximum-width wedge foot. The 200 µm length, which runs along the wire direction, holds two bond footprints, so a lifted bond can be redone on fresh Au [team calc; inference]. The outer edge of each row pad sits **120 µm from the die edge**, well inside EPFL's 500 µm limit (from `make_chip.py`: row centre at y = ±4780 µm on a 5000 µm half-die [team calc]). One figure in the brief, "pad ≥ 2-3× bond foot", does not appear as a rule in any verified source. The closest verified guidance is facility minimums of 55-60 µm against a standard of 100 µm, about 1.3-1.5× a 75 µm ball.

The weak point is the clearance around the pads, not their size. In `make_chip.py`, `GAP_FAR = 10` sets a 10 µm gap from every electrode outside the trap core to the Au ground fill. The fill therefore runs as a **40 µm finger between neighbouring pads and a 10 µm strip just outboard of each pad row**, and every bond wire crosses that strip. Measured in the GDS, the nearest other Au to pads TL2, TR5, RF and C is exactly **10.0 µm** [team calc]. That puts a grounded conductor 10 µm away on four sides, while EPFL treats 60 µm as the minimum pad-to-pad spacing. A bond that lands 33 µm off centre, or a squash that spreads that far, shorts a DC electrode to ground. On the RF pad the same short kills the resonator [team calc: 22.5 µm margin + 10 µm gap; inference]. This short can be fixed in the layout. A ≥ 30 µm keep-out removes both the fingers and the outboard strip and leaves the 60 µm pad-to-pad gap as the only spacing.

## Published traps use 95-300 µm pads on about a 100 µm pitch, and most papers do not say

Only two trap papers in the notes give on-die pad dimensions explicitly. Sandia's Phoenix and Peregrine traps (HOA platform) use pads that "are 95 um wide with a 5 um gap to each neighbor". They sit only on the long ends of the bowtie, with "no bondpads down the center isthmus of the trap to allow access for laser beams". The RF electrode gets **seven wire bonds** and each control electrode one, into a custom 102-pin AlN CPGA/CLGA ([Revelle, arXiv:2009.02398, Sec. 5.3, Sec. 6.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02398)). Leibrandt et al. bonded to "300 x 300 um^2 Al pads ... at the edge of the chip" on a 1.0 × 1.0 cm die in a 100-pin CPGA on a 1.27 mm alumina spacer ([Leibrandt et al., arXiv:0904.2599](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.2599)). Several other designs specify the bond rather than the pad. Stick et al. wedge-bonded 12.5 × 75 µm Au ribbons to pads on M1, 9 µm below the electrode plane, and pulled them taut ([arXiv:1008.0990](https://arxiv.org/abs/1008.0990)). Tabakov et al. kept loops below 30 µm above the electrode plane ([arXiv:1501.06554](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.06554)). GTRI and A*STAR removed bond pads from the trap die altogether, using TSVs and an interposer ([Guise et al., arXiv:1412.5576](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.5576); [Zhao et al., arXiv:2101.00869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.00869)). The cryogenic and photonic traps closest to ours give no pad dimensions in the text: NIST's trap-integrated SNSPD ([Todaro et al., arXiv:2008.00065](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.00065)), MIT LL's photonic trap ([Niffenegger et al., arXiv:2001.05052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05052)) and ETH's ([Mehta et al., arXiv:2002.02258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02258)).

Our pads therefore sit between the 2020 Sandia design and the 2009 MIT design, and at 37-39 connections our pad count is far below the ~100-pin CPGA standard that sets the counts elsewhere [inference]. The Sandia practice we have not copied is bonding the RF electrode with several wires. A 200 × 200 µm pad has room for that: it holds a 2 × 2 or 2 × 3 grid of 25 µm wedge feet at the ESCC dimensions [team calc].

## The pads are the smallest parasitic on the die

The capacitance figures are fringe-inclusive [team calc] estimates, computed from parallel-plate and Hammerstad microstrip formulas ([Wikipedia, Microstrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstrip)). The inputs are 6 µm Au over 10 µm PECVD SiO₂ with ε_r = 4.2, above the Al plane; lateral capacitance to the fill is not included. On those numbers the **RF pad is 0.17 pF**, the 60 µm × 2.7 mm RF lead 0.74 pF, and the two 84 µm RF rails at least 2.5 pF. Each **DC pad is 0.11 pF** against 0.32-0.51 pF for its 6 µm × 5-8 mm lead. Each SNSPD lead is ~3.4 pF, because it runs over Al through only 238 nm of SiO₂, while an SNSPD pad would be ~0.08 pF even if Al lay under it. The published budgets give scale. Phoenix allows 7 ± 1 pF for the die and 4 ± 1 pF for the CPGA ([arXiv:2009.02398, Sec. 8.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02398)). Siverns et al. measured 17 ± 2 pF for a trap plus vacuum system ([arXiv:1106.5013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.5013.pdf)), and the Sussex lattice chip alone was 19 ± 1 pF ([Sterling et al. 2014](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1302.3781)). Dissipation goes as P ≈ ½ R ω² C² V² ([arXiv:2009.02398, Sec. 4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02398)), and dielectric loss as G = ωC tan δ ([Hughes et al., arXiv:1101.3207](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1101.3207)). At 53 V and 28 MHz the RF pad alone dissipates **0.04-0.37 mW** for tan δ = 10⁻³-10⁻², and shifts a 20 pF resonator by about 0.4 % in frequency [team calc]. Halving the RF pad to 100 × 100 µm saves 0.11 pF, about 3 % of the on-chip total, and costs the room for multiple RF bonds [team calc]. When the literature says "pads cost RF capacitance" it means designs with no ground plane and large pad areas. Zhao et al. cut RF-to-ground capacitance from 32 to 3 pF by removing pads and connecting circuits from a Si die that had no ground plane ([arXiv:2101.00869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.00869)). That case does not apply to a die whose Al plane already screens the Si [inference].

On the DC electrodes, a pad's capacitance to the buried Al acts as a small built-in RF shunt. Enlarging a DC pad from 120 × 200 to 200 × 200 µm would cut RF pickup by only 10-15 % [team calc]. GTRI's dedicated on-chip capacitors were 60 pF per electrode, about 100× the pad-plus-lead value, and the authors call such capacitors "an essential element for reducing RF pickup" that must sit close to the trap ([Doret et al., arXiv:1204.4147, Sec. 2.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.4147)). Pad area is not a useful filter knob. Whether our filter-capacitor-free DC lines pick up too much RF depends on DC-to-RF coupling capacitances nobody has computed for this layout. With GTRI's 1.4 fF coupling, the illustrative estimate is ~150 mV of RF on a DC electrode [team calc].

For the SNSPD, the reset time is L_k/R_L, about 55-66 ns for a ~3 µH NbN meander into 50 Ω [team calc]. Latching is set by L_k/R_L against hotspot cooling ([Annunziata et al., arXiv:1008.0895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.0895)). The ~3.4 pF lead capacitance leaves the circuit about 100× overdamped, so neither pad nor lead capacitance changes the reset or latching [team calc]. The SNSPD risk is RF pickup, about 9.3 µA per fF of coupling to the RF electrode at 53 V and 28 MHz [team calc]. Todaro et al. kept their leads narrow for exactly this reason ([arXiv:2008.00065, Supplement](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00065)). Hampel et al. moved the leads several hundred µm from the RF and put a *grounded* mirror under them ([arXiv:2302.01462](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.01462)). Our SNSPD pads are ~9.5 mm from the RF pad, so their size is a pure bonding choice [team calc; inference].

Breakdown at 53 V does not depend on pad area either. Bulk breakdown through 10 µm SiO₂ is out of reach, and surface flashover across the gaps is the limit. That limit is about 2.5× below bulk breakdown ([Hughes et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1101.3207)), drops about 30 % with a conductor under the gap ([Sterling et al. 2013, arXiv:1208.5672](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1208.5672)), and measured hundreds of V to ~1 kV in microfabricated traps ([Sterling et al. 2014](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1302.3781)). Flashover voltage scales roughly as √gap ([Hughes et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1101.3207)), so widening the RF pad's 10 µm gap to the fill to 30 µm raises its flashover margin by about 1.7× [team calc], on top of removing the placement short above.

## What lies under the pad decides whether bonding breaks it

The one trap failure in the notes that is attributed to bond pads is about the layer under them. Leibrandt et al. saw shorts between electrodes during bakeout or after weeks of trapping. They blamed "the thin silicon nitride insulating layer", which "is especially stressed beneath the wire bonding pads", and recommended SiO₂ instead ([arXiv:0904.2599](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.2599)). IC work on Al pads says the same thing more sharply: "a full sheet of metal in a pad sub-layer will dominate the top SiO2 cracking performance", slotting it gives a "dramatic improvement", and a crack becomes a leakage path between nodes ([Hunter et al., onsemi](https://www.onsemi.com/site/pdf/UseofHarshWaferProbingtoEvaluateVariousBondPadStructures.pdf)). GTRI bonded pads over 10 µm PECVD SiO₂ on an Al plane that appears to be continuous, and reported no failures from it ([Doret et al., Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.4147)). Our stack is the same pad-over-insulator-over-Al geometry. The GDS shows **85 % Al coverage under a row pad and 100 % under the RF pad** [team calc]. But the insulator is SiO₂, not Si₃N₄, and it is 10 µm thick, about 10× the ~1 µm inter-level SiO₂ in the onsemi study, under 6 µm Au rather than < 2 µm Al. The cracking risk should therefore be much lower than either precedent, but it is not zero [inference]. The design already cuts the Al out under the SNSPD pads, and an earlier layout also cut it out under the test pads to prevent shorts through thin SiO₂ (DESIGN_LOG). If the short-loop test shows pad-to-Al leakage after bonding, the next revision can cut the Al out under the DC pads at almost no cost: about 0.1 pF of shunt per electrode [team calc]. The RF pad should keep its Al, because removing it exposes the RF pad to room-temperature loss in the high-resistivity Si ([Romaszko et al., arXiv:1908.00267, Sec. 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.00267v3)) [inference].

The pad's own stiffness and hardness matter too. Au thermosonic bonds over thick polyimide improved when Ti was added under the pad, which the authors attributed to "increased bond pad rigidity" ([IEEE record via infona.pl](https://www.infona.pl/resource/bwmeta1.element.ieee-art-000000187617), abstract only). A harder pad protects against cratering ([DeepDyve, Cu ball-bond cratering](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/springer-journals/cratering-on-thermosonic-copper-wire-ball-bonding-AdLtMnCiQl), excerpt only), but the pad's top surface still has to be soft enough to bond. Wire-bond-grade electroplated Au is the soft, high-purity class, about ≤ 90 Knoop ([ASTM B488](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/astm/cd877f37-2151-48d2-9883-32439fc575e8/astm-b488-012010e1); [Gleco Plating](https://glecoplating.com/?p=221), vendor excerpt), and annealing softens plated Au ([US 8021976](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8021976)). At 6 µm, our Au is far above the 100-200 nm thickness minimums ([EPFL CMi](https://www.epfl.ch/research/facilities/cmi/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/HB10_guidelines.pdf)). Its hardness depends on the MIT.nano plating bath, which no one has checked.

The recessed bond sites are the least covered by any source. Recessed-pad wedge tools are designed for pads **2-8 µm** below the surrounding surface ([US7451905B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7451905)). Our Al M1 openings and SNSPD openings sit 10 µm down, and no source gives a clearance rule linking opening width to tool geometry. The 170 × 170 µm M1 openings fit even the longest acceptable wedge foot. The SNSPD openings are 120 µm along the wire direction (the wires leave the left die edge along −x) and 90 µm across. A foot near the ESCC length limit of 125 µm would not fit, and the 90 µm width leaves about 7 µm per side for a 75 µm-wide foot [team calc]. In geometry terms, a ball-bond capillary is wider than its ~75 µm ball and may land on the SiO₂ rim of a 90 µm well before the ball is fully formed [inference]. The SNSPD pad is also the thinnest bond surface on the die, about 350 nm Ti/Au. That is within EPFL's 100 nm and UC Davis's 200 nm minimums, but thinner pads are more easily damaged ([US 8198737](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8198737), patent background). EPFL also warns that ball bonding's electronic flame-off spark makes it unsuitable for "electrically sensitive samples" ([EPFL CMi](https://www.epfl.ch/research/facilities/cmi/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/HB10_guidelines.pdf)). An open NbN meander is such a sample [inference]. Finally, for the Al M1 openings, Au-Al intermetallic growth is a bake problem, not a 4 K one. Sandia measured Au-wire-on-Al-pad failure at 384 ± 58 h at 200 °C ([Haltli et al., arXiv:2402.12546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12546)) and caps bakes at 200 °C for under 7 days ([arXiv:2009.02398, Sec. 5.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02398)). The traveler's plan of Al wedge wire on Al openings and Au wire on Au pads avoids the issue.

## Optical access depends on wire placement, not pad size

Every trap source that discusses optics frames the issue as where the wires go and how high they rise. Sandia keeps "the trap center ... clear of wirebonds to enable laser beams to be introduced along the linear axis" ([arXiv:2009.02398, Sec. 5.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02398)). Tabakov et al. kept loops low and placed pads at the perimeter "to reduce scattering of the lasers from the wirebonds" ([arXiv:1501.06554](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.06554)). Romaszko et al. list "wirebonds do not impede the optical path" as a design requirement, because scatter "will drastically affect the ability to perform experiments" ([arXiv:1908.00267](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00267)). GTRI saw an 8 µm-waist beam begin to clip on the CPGA edge rather than on the wires ([arXiv:1412.5576](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.5576)), which makes the carrier and spacer height the next optical constraint after the wires. On this die the 45° beams through either zone pass **about 0.58 mm in-plane from the nearest corner of a pad row**, the inner corner at (±3560, ±4680) µm [team calc]. The wires leave perpendicular to the edge, away from the beam, so the four rows are clear. One choice does conflict with Sandia's practice. The RF, centre-DC and SNSPD bonds leave the side edges at y ≈ 0, directly on the trap axis. They do not affect the planned 45° beams, but they rule out an axial beam later [inference]. No source quantifies scatter against wire distance or gives a keep-out distance in µm.

## Gaps and sources that remain unverified

Several inputs could not be confirmed and should not be quoted as fact. The MIT.nano bonder models, wire stock, capillary tip diameter and wedge foot length are all unknown; no public MIT.nano page was found. The USC wiki numbers for an F&S bonder are self-described as LLM-generated ([USC Nanofab wiki](https://wiki.nanofab.usc.edu/index.php/Ball_%26_Wedge_bonder)). The UC Davis, ESCC, ProQuest, Circuits Assembly, Cu-cratering and Gleco figures were read only as search excerpts. The MIL-STD-883 TM 2010/2017 bond criteria, Harman's *Wire Bonding in Microelectronics* and the manufacturers' design guides were not read. The Sandia HOA-2 report ([OSTI 1237003](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1237003)) and Sterling et al.'s flashover-versus-gap figure were not extracted. A search snippet about Sandia "80 um wirebonds" has no verified source. No paper was found for **"Dietl 2025"**, which `fab/test_plan.md` cites for a 120-needle probe card; treat that reference as unverified until someone locates it. There is also no measured literature on wire-bond survival through 300 K to 4 K cycling, on bonding over thick PECVD SiO₂ above a buried plane, or on yield versus pad size for manual bonding. Finally, the ~0.1-0.17 pF pad and ≥ 3.4 pF on-chip RF figures leave out lateral capacitance to the fill across 5-10 µm gaps. That term grows when the gaps are narrow and the Au is 6 µm thick, so the numbers are lower bounds.

## Conclusion

Pad area turns out to be a nearly free variable on this die. Electrically, the leads and RF rails dominate. Mechanically, the stiff 10 µm SiO₂ / 6 µm Au stack spreads the load. Optically, only wire placement matters. That makes clearance and bondability the deciding criteria, and shifts attention from the pad to the 10 µm around it and the layers beneath it. The one real design flaw the literature check exposes is the fill geometry: a grounded Au conductor 10 µm from every pad converts ordinary hand-bonding misplacement into electrode-to-ground shorts. The second-largest risk sits on the two SNSPD pads, which combine the thinnest Au, the narrowest well and the most damage-sensitive device on the chip.

The literature cannot settle whether bonding through 10 µm PECVD SiO₂ cracks it onto the Al. The only precedent for that geometry is GTRI's, and it reports no failures and no measurements. That makes a bond-and-leak test on the real stack the most valuable test available before R1. It costs a few test-die pads and decides whether the next revision needs Al cut-outs under the DC pads.
