## Q1. Which geometry option minimizes fab risk while keeping ~saturated internal efficiency at 493 nm?

### Takeaway
The lowest-risk choice that still has strong literature support for saturated internal efficiency at visible wavelengths is to keep an EBL-written NbN nanowire meander. Ranked by risk reduction per unit effort, the fixes are: (1) relax fill factor at the turnarounds and round the hairpins; (2) widen the wire modestly (about 150–200 nm), which cuts the square count and raises Isw; (3) use a positive-tone resist (ZEP or PMMA, developed without TMAH) or salty-developer HSQ; (4) add resistance-screening and Ic-screening test structures plus redundant detectors, because constriction yield falls with wire length. The current A30 has about 45,000 squares (4.5 mm of wire). That is about 9x longer than the 10 x 10 um devices that Kerman 2007 already found have lower constriction factors than 3 x 3 um devices. Yield, not latching, is A30's main electrical risk.

### Cited Findings
**Constrictions and length/area scaling (yield)**
- Kerman et al., "Constriction-limited detection efficiency of superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors," APL 90, 101110 (2007), DOI 10.1063/1.2696926, arXiv physics/0611260. Main devices: about 5 nm NbN on sapphire, 90 nm wire, 200 nm pitch (45% fill), 3 x 3.3 um or 10 x 10 um. Statistics: 132 devices on one chip (Fig. 1), 170 devices of 90 nm wire across two chips (Fig. 2a), and 310 more devices on a separate chip (Fig. 3c,d). — [ar5iv physics/0611260](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/physics/0611260); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0611260)
- Kerman 2007 defines the constriction factor C = Ic,obs / Ic (the superconducting fraction of the cross-section at the worst point). C can be measured electrically from the product Ic,obs x Rn, which is independent of wire cross-section (Fig. 2b,c, slope-1 line through the origin). The authors call this a potentially important screening technique that needs no optical test. — [ar5iv physics/0611260](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/physics/0611260)
- Kerman 2007, Fig. 3c,d: 10 x 10 um devices have a lower average C than 3 x 3.3 um devices, "qualitatively consistent with some fixed area density of constrictions." The authors expect the yield of high-efficiency devices of 10 x 10 um or larger to be "quite low." SEM showed no lithographic errors in severely constricted devices (C ~0.5), so they suspect thickness variations or material defects. — [ar5iv physics/0611260](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/physics/0611260)
- Charaev 2020 summarizes Gaudio et al.: in NbN, "the experimental switching current is observed to be continuously reduced with the increase of the length of the nanowire," which they attribute to intrinsic constrictions in polycrystalline films and to variation of the gap. Charaev says amorphous WSi and MoSi "appear to suffer from fewer constrictions." (Gaudio's paper itself was not opened.) — [Charaev et al., arXiv:2002.09088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.09088)
- NbN microstrip yield at scale (Xu et al. 2023, i-line stepper, 7 nm NbN): in 1, 2 and 3 um meanders, Isw fell as active area grew up to 1.5 x 1.5 mm, "because the larger the area of the device, the more likely the appearance of defects in the long microstrip." 3 um x 100 um reference microbridges: mean Isw 508 uA with sigma 45 uA (8.9%), and 67.2% (43/64) above the mean. Only 39.1% (25/64) of the 1 mm^2 SMSPDs met the Isw criterion for saturation at 532 nm. SEM confirmed "obvious defects" in the low-Isw large devices. — [Xu et al., arXiv:2304.06921](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)

**Fill factor and proximity at small gaps**
- Yang et al., "Suppressed critical current in SNSPDs with high fill-factors," IEEE TAS 19, 318 (2009). 100 devices on one chip, 90 nm wire (90 +/- 5 nm by SEM), fill factor 40% to 88%, gaps down to 12 nm. Ic of the 88%-fill devices was suppressed by 35% relative to the 40%-fill devices (Fig. 4b). The suppression was systematic, not random scatter, and not explained by width error: it would need 32 nm of width loss, which SEM did not show. At equal bias, higher-fill devices had higher DE, but they could not reach high bias. The best DE was 20%, at 60% fill. — [Yang et al. 2009, QNN PDF](https://qnn-rle.mit.edu/documents/yang-6-2009-60.pdf); [DSpace 1721.1/73188](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/73188)
- Yang 2009 process (no TMAH anywhere): 80 nm PMMA (2200k MW), baked at 160 C for 8 min, Raith 150 at 30 kV writing the *gaps* (positive tone). Cold develop in 3:1 IPA:MIBK for 25 s. NbN RIE at 100 W and 10 mT for 1 min, which left 40 nm of PMMA. Strip in NMP with ultrasonic agitation for 5 min. Aligned S1813 photolithography then masked the active region for a second RIE (Fig. 2). — [Yang 2009](https://qnn-rle.mit.edu/documents/yang-6-2009-60.pdf)
- Charaev 2020 patterned 1 and 3 um MoSi microwires with positive-tone ZEP 520A: 335 nm thick (5000 rpm), 125 keV, 500 uC/cm^2, developed in o-xylene at 0 C for 90 s, then CF4 RIE at 50 W for 6 min. It used fill factor 23% "to minimize the current crowding." — [Charaev 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.09088)
- Akhlaghi 2012 (8 nm NbTiN, HSQ at 125 keV, TMAH develop, Ar ion milling) noted that the smallest fabricated "sharp" inner radius was about 35 nm, and this radius was uncontrolled. Variation in Ic was larger for sharper bends. Because a meander has many 180-degree turns in series, "having at least one very sharp bend [is] quite possible," which they offer as an explanation for low SNSPD yield. — [Akhlaghi et al., arXiv:1205.4290](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.4290)
- Clem & Berggren 2011, Eq. (175), as relayed by the summarizer (it was not read directly and should be checked): an Ic-vs-fill-factor suppression Ic(f)/Ic(0) = (1 + 2f - 3f^2)^(1/6) / (1 + f)^(1/3). This gives about 0.91 at f = 0.5 and about 0.95 at f = 1/3. — [ar5iv 1109.4881](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1109.4881)

**Visible-wavelength saturation in a near-identical NbN film (gives the EBL-free option a target, see Q2)**
- Xu 2023: 7 nm NbN, Rsq(20 K) = 656 ohm/sq, Tc = 7.8 K, D = 0.48 cm^2/s, Isw/Idep = 0.68 at 2.2 K. Saturated IDE at 532 nm and near-saturated up to 800 nm (IDE 99.8% at 800 nm from a logistic fit), measured at 0.85 K. — [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)

### Inferences
- `[team calc]` A30 wire length is 150 x 30 um = 4.5 mm = 45,000 squares at 100 nm. Kerman's 10 x 10 um device at 200 nm pitch has about 50 lines x 10 um = 0.5 mm, so A30 is about 9x longer, and B60 is about 18x longer. Kerman's own wording ("quite low" yield at 10 x 10 um and above) implies that for MIT-style NbN, several A30 detectors per run, plus electrical pre-screening, will be needed to get one good device. This is about statistics, not about the design being wrong.
- `[inference]` Options ranked by fab-risk reduction against effort, for 493 nm, all on EBL:
  1. **Keep 100 nm, but turn the fill factor down at the turnarounds only.** Use a bulb or flared hairpin with a gap of about 2W at the turn and keep f = 0.5 in the straight sections. Also round the inner corner. This is a GDS-only change (see Q4).
  2. **Widen the wire to 150–200 nm at the same 50% fill.** Square count falls by 1.5–2x (to about 30,000 or 22,500 squares for 30 x 30 um). Isw rises about proportionally to width, so the absolute margin against RF-induced current improves. A given absolute width error (LER of a few nm) becomes a smaller fraction of the width. Larger gaps (150–200 nm) also reduce proximity-effect bridging in negative-tone HSQ. The costs: wider wires make sharp corners relatively worse (Clem's R falls with xi/W, Q4), and saturation at 493 nm needs a slightly higher Isw/Idep. No source read here measured 150–300 nm NbN at 493 nm (see Gaps). Korneeva's 0.53–5 um NbN bridges detecting single 408 nm photons (Q2) make 150–200 nm low-risk physically.
  3. **Switch resist tone.** ZEP or PMMA develop in o-xylene or MIBK:IPA, which removes TMAH from the NbN process completely (this is a stated constraint, and Toomey 2018 in nbn_process.md shows TMAH thins NbN). You write the gaps, not the wire. The trade-off is a weaker etch mask (Yang's PMMA lost half its thickness in 1 min of RIE, which is fine for 5 nm NbN). Positive tone also leaves the unwritten NbN field in place unless a second photolithography step and etch clears it, as in Yang's Fig. 2(e). If the team keeps HSQ, it needs a non-TMAH developer (NaOH/NaCl "salty" developer; its effect on NbN is unreported, per nbn_process.md Q4).
  4. **Do not go to higher fill factor** (Yang: -35% Ic at 88%), and do not use SNAPs or parallel arrays (Q2 Gaps). Both add fab burden with no benefit for a 30 um ion-fluorescence detector.
- `[inference]` Write time is not a constraint at this size. `[team calc]` A30 wire area is 4.5 mm x 0.1 um = 450 um^2 = 4.5e-6 cm^2. At an HSQ-type dose of about 2 mC/cm^2 (Hortensius used about 2 mC/cm^2 at 100 keV), that is about 9 nC, or about 9 s at 1 nA. Leads, dummy fill and the dose array add minutes at most. The whole meander fits in one EBL field, so stitching is not an issue either.
- `[team calc]` Room-temperature resistance screening: R_RT is about N_sq x Rs = 45,000 x ~600 ohm/sq, about 27 Mohm for A30 (about 54 Mohm for B60). An open circuit (broken wire) reads as infinite. A short from HSQ bridging between adjacent lines skips at most two 30 um lines per bridge (about 600 squares, about 1.3% of R). So two-point RT probing catches opens and gross multi-bridge shorts, but a single bridge needs about 1% comparison to a same-chip reference meander. It cannot see thickness constrictions at all: that needs cold Ic x Rn (Kerman 2007).
- `[inference]` Test strategy that follows from the sources: on the T8 / test die, put (a) short straight wires and microbridges of the same width as Ic references (Xu used 100 um bridges); (b) meanders with sharp vs rounded hairpins at the same fill factor (Akhlaghi's paired-device method: devices a few um apart, compared as ratios); (c) meanders of 2–3 lengths, to measure Ic vs length on the team's own film. Screen at room temperature by resistance, then at 4 K and below by Ic x Rn against the straight-wire reference before optical tests.

## Q2. Is EBL avoidable (photolithographic wide-wire or microstrip NbN detector), and what does it cost in efficiency or dark counts?

### Takeaway
In principle, yes. NbN microstrips 1–3 um wide, made by i-line stepper photolithography in a film almost identical to the MIT room-temperature NbN (7 nm, 656 ohm/sq, Tc 7.8 K), showed saturated IDE at 532 nm. But that result needed (i) operation at 0.85 K, (ii) Isw/Idep of about 0.68 or more, (iii) a cold shunt resistor of about 2–6 ohm (or an on-chip series inductor) to stop latching, and (iv) acceptance of lower fill factor (0.33) and lower yield. Korneeva's NbN bridges (0.53–5 um) detect single 408 nm photons at 1.7 K and 4.2 K, but without a clean plateau. The WSi and MoSi microwire successes ran at 0.3–0.8 K. If the trap cryostat runs at about 3–4 K, a microstrip NbN detector is a research risk, not a simplification. Maskless lithography at about 1 um was not shown in any source read here.

### Cited Findings
**NbN microbridges (Korneeva et al., PR Applied 9, 064037 (2018), arXiv:1802.02881)**
- Film: 5.8 nm NbN by DC reactive magnetron sputtering at 400 C, on 250 nm SiO2 on Si. Tc about 8.3 K, 0.88 A/s. Bridge widths 0.53–5.15 um, constriction-type "neck" with edges as circular arcs (radius 8.6 um) to avoid current crowding. Patterned by **EBL** and RIE, not by photolithography (Appendix A). — [Korneeva 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.02881)
- Table I: jc(4.2 K) = 2.3–3.8 MA/cm^2, about 0.5–0.7 of the calculated jdep (abstract: "about 50%"); resistivity about 390–430 uOhm cm. — [Korneeva 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.02881)
- Single-photon response (linear in photon flux, Poisson inter-arrival times) at 408, 829 and 1550 nm for all widths up to 5.15 um. Two regimes: Regime I is fluctuation-assisted (IDE slope equal to the dark-count slope); Regime II is deterministic. "We also do not observe a saturation of IDE," which they attribute to the neck geometry. Model-based claim of "near-unity intrinsic probability at the short wavelengths." The detection current density jdet is about 0.5 jdep at 408 nm and about 0.7 jdep at 1550 nm (Sec. V, Figs. 6–7). — [Korneeva 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.02881)
- Temperature: at 4.2 K (about 0.5 Tc), the widest sample (5.15 um) detected only in the fluctuation-assisted regime, while at 1.7 K it also reached the deterministic regime (Sec. IV). — [Korneeva 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.02881)
- Latching: "these relatively wide samples have impractically high critical currents"; a 3 ohm shunt resistor was connected in parallel to prevent latching. Lk = 0.4–1.1 nH. — [Korneeva 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.02881)
- Worth reading: Sec. II and Table I (jc/jdep), Fig. 6 (IDE vs I/Idep at 4.2 K and 1.7 K), Fig. 7 (jdet independent of width), Appendix A (film).

**NbN SMSPD by i-line photolithography (Xu et al. 2023, arXiv:2304.06921; journal version not checked)**
- Process: 7 nm NbN by DC magnetron sputtering (8-inch Nb target, under 3.5% thickness variation over 4 in) on thermal SiO2/Si. Resist AZ703 about 0.8 um. Canon FPA-3030i5+ i-line stepper (0.35 um resolution), then RIE. "Only one step of exposure and etching is needed," which defines strips and pads together. Meanders at fill factor 0.33 "to reduce the current-crowding effect in the bends"; spirals at 0.5. — [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)
- 2.2 K microbridges: Isw 127.1 +/- 2.5, 258.3 +/- 3.8 and 390.2 +/- 5.2 uA for 1, 2 and 3 um. Tc 7.3–7.9 K. — [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)
- UV vs EBL line edges: LER about 14.3 nm (UV) vs about 5.5 nm (EBL). A linear fit of Isw vs width has an x-intercept "residual strip width" of 34.7 +/- 3.3 nm (about 17 nm per edge), consistent with the UV LER. An EBL-tailored 0.99 um bridge cut from a 2.97 um UV bridge had the same jsw (18.66 vs 18.77 mA/um^2, within 0.6%). There was no Rsq or Tc change from the EBL re-processing. — [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)
- Operation: 0.85 K sorption-stage cryocooler. Shunt resistors of about 5.8 ohm (1 um) or 2.0 ohm (3 um) "to prevent latching," soldered on a PCB about 8 mm from the chip. The 1 mm^2 device had Isw of about 467 uA unshunted at 2.2 K and about 610 uA shunted at 0.85 K. — [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)
- Performance: saturated IDE at 532 nm, at about 500 uA (0.82 of shunted Isw); estimated IDE 99.8% at 800 nm and 97.5% at 850 nm. SDE at 1550 nm about 5–7% at a DCR of about 300 cps; intrinsic DCR (fiber removed) over 15x lower below 0.9 Isw. Timing jitter 102–144 ps. — [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)
- Worth reading: Sec. 2 (process, one-mask flow), Sec. 4 and Fig. 3 (UV vs EBL LER and jsw), Sec. 5 and Fig. 4 (yield maps), Fig. 5(d) (DE vs bias at 532–1550 nm).

**WSi and MoSi microwires (amorphous films, sub-1 K)**
- Chiles et al., "Superconducting microwire detectors based on WSi with single-photon sensitivity in the near-infrared," APL 116, 242602 (2020), DOI 10.1063/5.0006221 (per NIST export), arXiv:2002.12858. WSi on Si with 160 nm thermal SiO2, at 76 mm wafer scale. "Most of the devices were patterned with conventional 365 nm photolithography," with etch in dilute SF6/Ar. Measured at 750–800 mK. "On-chip meandered inductors (with wires significantly wider than the width of the device under test) are used to prevent latching." — [Chiles 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.12858); [NIST record](https://www.nist.gov/publications/superconducting-microwire-detectors-single-photon-sensitivity-near-infrared)
- Chiles: Jsw/Jdep is lower for widths under 700 nm and "mostly saturates for larger wires, most likely due to small constrictions from photolithographic patterning." Wider devices exceed 0.55 Jdep even with 400 um length and a hairpin. Plateaus at 1310 nm (and 1550 nm for the thinner films) in photolithographic out-and-back wires up to 1.25 um. Note that the 3–4 um devices and the 2 um, 362 x 362 um meander with a 1550 nm plateau came from a separate **e-beam** run (Fig. 4). — [Chiles 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.12858)
- Charaev et al., "Large-area microwire MoSi single-photon detectors at 1550 nm wavelength," APL 116, 242603 (2020), arXiv:2002.09088 (MIT QNN). 3 nm MoSi (Tc about 4.1 K), with a 2 nm Si cap in situ; 1 and 3 um wires; up to 400 x 400 um; fill 23%; patterned by **EBL** (ZEP). Saturated IDE at 1550 nm at 300 mK. DCR below 1e3 cps at 99% Isw. Isw 8–10 uA (1 um) and 27–30 uA (3 um); jc/jdep 0.5–0.63. The 5 nm MoSi devices of the same geometry did *not* detect single near-IR photons. — [Charaev 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.09088)

### Inferences
- `[inference]` What an EBL-free NbN detector would need here:
  - **Cold enough stage.** The visible-wavelength saturation in NbN microstrips (Xu) was at 0.85 K, and Korneeva's widest bridge lost the deterministic regime at 4.2 K. If the trap cryostat base is about 3–4 K (Hampel's trap-integrated NbTiN ran at 2.6–6 K, per nbn_process.md), a 1 um NbN microstrip is unlikely to plateau. Photon energy at 493 nm (2.5 eV) is between the 408 nm and 532 nm points, which helps.
  - **High Isw/Idep (about 0.6–0.7 or more).** The MIT room-temperature film's Isw/Idep in wide strips is unknown. A same-chip microbridge measurement would be the first test.
  - **Latching mitigation (Q3).** A 30 x 30 um, 1 um-wide meander is a few hundred squares, far below the inductance needed at 50 ohm, so it needs a cold shunt (about 2–6 ohm near the chip) or an on-chip series inductor. The inductor needs a long, narrower-than-bond-pad NbN meander, which brings back the long-wire yield issue at a coarser width.
  - **Efficiency cost.** Fill factor drops to about 0.33 (vs 0.5), lowering NbN absorption in the window stack (the 0.40 at 493 nm in design/CLAUDE.md is for the current fill), unless the optical stack is re-optimized. LER of about 14 nm costs only about 3% of a 1 um width.
  - **Dark counts.** Reported microwire DCRs are about 300 cps (Xu, with fiber) and under 1e3 cps (Charaev, MoSi). That is probably acceptable against ion-fluorescence count rates, but the team should compare against its own expected collected photon rate.
- `[inference]` Lithography resolution at MIT.nano: a 1 um wire with 2 um gaps is near the limit of typical maskless aligners. The i-line stepper used by Xu (0.35 um) and the 365 nm contact lithography used by Chiles were not maskless. Whether MIT.nano has an i-line stepper or a maskless writer with about 1 um or better fidelity on NbN was not checked `[verify]`.
- `[inference]` A hybrid is cheaper than either extreme: photolithography for the leads, pads and the wide on-chip inductor, and EBL only for the 30 x 30 um active meander. This is what Xu's "in situ tailoring" and Chiles's inductor practice imply. It shrinks the EBL job but keeps one EBL step.
- `[inference]` SNAPs and parallel arrays are the wrong direction here. They need narrower wires (20–40 nm per the excerpts), extra series chokes or resistors, and more lithography. Their benefits (signal-to-noise, Isw, photon-number resolution) matter little for a single ion-fluorescence pixel.

### Gaps
- No NbN microstrip result was found at about 493 nm and 3–4 K with a clear plateau.
- Korneeva's later work ("Influence of sheet resistance and strip width on the detection efficiency saturation in micron-wide superconducting strips and large-area meanders," SuST 34, 084001 (2021), cited in Xu) was not opened; it is likely the most directly relevant follow-up.
- Vodolazov PR Applied 2017 (kinetic-equation theory, jdet/jdep vs temperature and wavelength) was not opened; only cited via Charaev and Korneeva.
- Whether a 1 um NbN strip from the MIT room-temperature film (5 nm, about 600 ohm/sq) reaches Isw/Idep of 0.6 or more is untested.

## Q3. What is the minimum wire length / kinetic inductance before latching at 50 ohm, and is A30 above it?

### Takeaway
With a 50 ohm readout, devices of only about 50 nH were measured to work without latching (MIT/Lincoln 5 nm NbN on sapphire, 3 x 3.3 um, Ic about 22–24 uA, tau_e = L/R about 1 ns). Latching set in when the load was raised (tau_e shortened) by about 5x or more. A30 has about 45,000 squares, `[team calc]` about 4.7 uH at about 105 pH/sq. That is about 100x above the 50 nH working point, so A30 is not near latching. The price is a reset time constant of about 95 ns (1/e), about 190 ns for B60. This is irrelevant at ion-fluorescence count rates. Latching only becomes a real constraint for the wide-wire or microstrip options (Q2), where L is tens of nH and Isw is hundreds of uA. Those need a shunt or a series inductor.

### Cited Findings
- Kerman et al., "Electrothermal feedback in superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors," PRB 79, 100509 (2009), arXiv:0812.0290. Mechanism: a correctly working device needs *unstable* electrothermal feedback, which "happens naturally through the slow electrical response associated with their relatively large kinetic inductance." Speeding up the reset (smaller L or larger RL) makes the feedback stable and the device latches. Recovery time constant tau_e = L/RL. — [Kerman 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.0290)
- Kerman 2009, Fig. 1(c–f): 3 um x 3.3 um devices with L of about 50 nH. With RS = 0 (RL = 50 ohm), Iswitch = Ic (no latching). Adding series RS (RL = 50 + RS, up to 250 ohm shown, Fig. 1d) reduces Ilatch below Ic, and DE at 0.975 Iswitch drops sharply with "speedup" (RS + 50)/50 (Fig. 1e,f). — [Kerman 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.0290)
- Kerman 2009, Figs. 2–3: 66 devices on 3 chips, keeping only unconstricted wires with Ic within 10% (typically 22–24 uA). Ranges RL = 20–1000 ohm and L = 6–600 nH, using a 3 um x 100 nm hotspot section in series with a 200 nm-wide meander inductor. The latching boundary follows tau_e/tau_th proportional to (Ilatch/Iss)^2 when tau_e >> tau_a. Fitted tau_a = 1.9 ns, tau_c = 7.7 ns. The stable-hotspot condition always requires Rss above roughly RL. — [Kerman 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.0290)
- Kerman 2009 film: 5 nm NbN on R-plane sapphire at 800 C, R about 500 ohm/sq, EBL in HSQ, Ti on-chip resistors. The authors note that raising the heat-transfer coefficient h to the substrate moves the wire "further into the unstable region," allowing faster reset without latching. — [Kerman 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.0290)
- Charaev 2020: "In order to prevent latching, we designed microwires with a total number of squares 10^4 and 7x10^3 for width of 1 and 3 um." Reset time 75 ns for 10^4 squares gives Lk = 375 pH/sq for 3 nm MoSi, so about 3.75 uH (1 um) at Isw 8–10 uA and about 2.6 uH (3 um) at Isw 27–30 uA. — [Charaev 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.09088)
- Korneeva 2018: 0.4–1.1 nH microbridges needed a 3 ohm shunt. Xu 2023: 1–3 um NbN SMSPDs needed 2–6 ohm shunts. Xu also notes the unshunted Isw is read low "due to latching." — [Korneeva 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.02881); [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921)
- Chiles 2020: on-chip meandered NbN-type inductors (WSi, wider wires than the detector) prevent latching. — [Chiles 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.12858)
- Measured reference for a trap-integrated device (from nbn_process.md, Hampel 2023): 20 x 30 um NbTiN, 1.3 uH, about 25 ns decay, worked at 50 ohm with Isw about 55–60 uA. — [Hampel et al., arXiv:2302.01462](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.01462)

### Inferences
- `[team calc]` Lk,sq of about 105 pH/sq (BCS estimate from nbn_process.md: Rs about 600 ohm/sq, Tc about 7.9 K) gives:
  - A30: 45,000 sq, about 4.7 uH, L/50 ohm about 95 ns; about 3 tau, roughly 0.3 us, to recover about 95% of bias;
  - B60: about 90,000 sq, about 9.5 uH, about 190 ns;
  - a 30 x 30 um meander with 200 nm wire at 50% fill: about 75 lines x 30 um = 2.25 mm = 11,250 sq, about 1.2 uH, about 24 ns;
  - a 30 x 30 um, 1 um-wide microstrip at f = 0.33: about 10 lines x 30 um = 300 sq, about 30 nH, about 0.6 ns, with Isw of order 100 uA.
- `[inference]` Latching margin: Kerman's 50 nH at 50 ohm worked at Ic of about 23 uA on sapphire. Two things push the MIT room-temperature NbN on SiO2-over-Al toward latching: poorer phonon escape into amorphous SiO2 (lower h than sapphire; Kerman says higher h widens the margin) and higher Isw for wider wires. Even so, A30 (about 4.7 uH) and the 200 nm option (about 1.2 uH, comparable to Hampel's working 1.3 uH) have 20–100x margin. The 1 um microstrip (about 30 nH, about 100 uA) is below Kerman's working point at about 4x higher current, so it will latch at 50 ohm without a shunt or an added inductor. To match Charaev's latch-free condition (several uH), a 1 um NbN strip would need tens of thousands of squares, i.e. tens of mm of wire, which defeats the point.
- `[inference]` Reset time is not a design driver: ion-fluorescence detection collects well under 1e6 photons/s, so even a 0.3 us recovery means negligible dead-time loss. If anything, A30's length is "too long" for yield reasons (Q1), not for speed.

## Q4. What do rounded turns cost in layout complexity, and what do they buy in Ic and bias margin?

### Takeaway
Theory: a sharp 180-degree hairpin in the zero-gap limit has R = Ic/Ic,straight = (pi xi/W)^(1/2) (Clem & Berggren Eq. 56); with xi of about 5 nm and W = 100 nm that is about 0.4. Finite gaps and the 35–60 nm inner radius that real lithography leaves raise this. A fully optimal turnaround (R = 1) needs a gap of 2W at the turn (local fill 1/3); at f = 0.5, rounded turns reach only R of about 0.60–0.71 (Clem Fig. 8). Experiments: in 1 um NbTiN, sharp 90-degree corners cut Ic from 382–560 uA (straight or optimally rounded) to 280–292 uA. In 100 nm NbTiN, optimal bends raised Ic and cut dark counts "by orders of magnitude" at equal bias, with no change in photo-response. Layout cost is small in a scripted GDS: one parametric curve per hairpin, plus a local gap flare at the turns, or turns kept out of the illuminated region.

### Cited Findings
- Clem & Berggren, "Geometry-dependent critical currents in superconducting nanocircuits," PRB 84, 174510 (2011), arXiv:1109.4881. Read via ar5iv through a summarizer; equations were confirmed by number, but figure values were not seen directly. — [ar5iv 1109.4881](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1109.4881); [DSpace 1721.1/69832](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/69832)
  - Sharp 180-degree hairpin (Sec. VII.1, Eq. 56): R = (pi xi/a)^(1/2), where a is the strip width in the zero-gap limit; R is about 0.56 at xi = 0.1a and about 0.18 at xi = 0.01a. Finite-gap "intermediate" hairpin (Sec. VII.3, Eq. 63, Fig. 7): R rises monotonically as the gap grows and reaches 1 at g/2a = 1/2.
  - Optimal hairpin (Sec. VII.2, Eqs. 58–59, Fig. 6): inner boundary x(y) = (a/pi) ln[2 cos(pi y/a)], with a/2 = W. Minimum radius 2W/pi, about 0.64 W. This gives R = 1 for xi << W, but requires the gap between strips to be twice the strip width (fill factor 1/3).
  - Rounded hairpins at f = 1/2 (Sec. VII.4, Fig. 8): R = 0.599 (Fig. 8a) and R = 0.707 (Fig. 8b) for the two shapes shown. Fig. 9 plots R vs f, and R = 1 only at f = 1/3 in the limit given.
  - Sharp 90-degree turn (Sec. VIII, Eq. 108): R = (3/2)(pi xi/4a)^(1/3), about 0.64 at xi = 0.1a and about 0.30 at xi = 0.01a. Optimal 90-degree bend (Eqs. 111–113): minimum radius 4W/pi, about 1.27 W, R = 1.
- Hortensius et al., "Critical-current reduction in thin superconducting wires due to current crowding," APL 100, 182602 (2012), arXiv:1203.4253. 8 nm NbTiN (170 uOhm cm, xi about 7 nm, Tc 9.5 K, Rs 217 ohm), 150 nm HSQ, 100 keV, about 2 mC/cm^2, TMAH develop, SF6/O2 RIE. 1 um-wide, 20 um-long wires at 4.2 K, 1000 Ic ramps each (Fig. 3): straight and optimally rounded 382–560 uA; sharp 90-degree 280–292 uA; stub 358 uA. Fabricated "sharp" inner radius was about 60 nm. For that radius, theory predicted R of about 0.33, but they observed about 0.51 relative to the largest Ic. Sharp-corner devices have a *narrower* Ic distribution (the corner is the defined weakest link), as in Fig. 4b. — [Hortensius 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1203.4253)
- Akhlaghi et al., "Reduced dark counts in optimized geometries for superconducting nanowire single photon detectors," Opt. Express 20, 23610 (2012), arXiv:1205.4290. Material and process: 100 nm-wide, 8 nm NbTiN on SiO2/Si; HSQ at 125 keV; Ar ion milling; Tc 8.4 K. Geometries compared: paired 90-degree and 180-degree bends (sharp, 45-degree, smallest optimal, 2x-size optimal); 180-degree turns at 200 nm spacing (optimal vs sharp), 300 nm spacing (optimal), and 100 nm spacing (sharp vs circularly rounded with r = 50 nm). Results:
  - the sharpest bends had "considerably lower" Ic;
  - the bigger optimal bends were slightly better than the smallest (more tolerant of fabrication error);
  - at 100 nm spacing, where no optimal design exists, circular rounding "can improve the reduced critical current by a considerable factor" (Fig. 2; exact ratio not in text);
  - at equal bias and equal efficiency, optimal bends cut DCR "by orders of magnitude" (Fig. 3);
  - chip-to-chip Ic variation was about 20%.
  - Calculated sheet-current density at a 35 nm-radius sharp 90-degree corner is 1.7x that of the optimal bend.
  - Measurement detail: devices were biased through a 490 nH inductor. — [Akhlaghi 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.4290)
- Xu 2023 and Charaev 2020 both cut the fill factor (0.33 and 0.23) explicitly to limit bend current crowding in wide-wire meanders. — [Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06921); [Charaev 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.09088)

### Inferences
- `[team calc]` For MIT room-temperature NbN, xi is about sqrt(hbar D / Delta), about 5 nm (with D about 0.5 cm^2/s from Xu, and Delta about 1.2 meV from Tc about 7.9 K). Zero-gap sharp-hairpin bound for W = 100 nm: (pi x 0.05)^(1/2) is about 0.40; for W = 200 nm, about 0.28. A sharp 90-degree corner at W = 100 nm: 1.5 (pi x 0.05/4)^(1/3), about 0.51. These are lower bounds. A30's 100 nm gap and the roughly 35–60 nm inner radius that EBL actually leaves put the real factor somewhere between these and the f = 0.5 rounded values (about 0.6–0.7). The one way to know is the paired sharp-vs-rounded test structure.
- `[inference]` What this buys: if the meander's Isw is turn-limited to about 0.4–0.7 of the straight-wire Ic, the straight sections (where photons are absorbed) can only be biased to that fraction. Whether that still sits on the 493 nm plateau for 100 nm NbN is the untested point. Rounding also moves the switching point (and the dark counts, per Akhlaghi) away from the turns, so DCR vs bias measures the wire, not the defect.
- `[inference]` Layout cost options in `make_chip.py`-style gdstk code, cheapest first:
  1. **Circular inner radius at the existing 200 nm pitch.** Replace each square inner corner with a semicircle of radius equal to half the gap (50 nm), with the outer edge concentric. Akhlaghi shows this "considerably" improves on sharp at 100 nm spacing. A few lines of code; no change to active area or fill.
  2. **Flared ("bulb") turnarounds.** Keep f = 0.5 in the straight lines, but widen the gap to about 2W only within about 1–2 pitches of each turn, and use Clem's Eq. 58 contour for the inner edge. Costs a little extra width at the two meander edges (a few hundred nm per side), a small loss of active area, and slightly more polygon vertices.
  3. **Global f = 1/3.** Simplest code and R = 1 everywhere, but about 1/3 fewer wire lines in the window, lower absorption, and about 33% fewer squares (which helps yield).

  EBL-side cost: curves become many small trapezoids; at 100 nm this is routine. The written radius will differ from the drawn radius (Akhlaghi saw a minimum of about 35 nm), so include the corner variants in the T8 dose array.
- `[inference]` Since the 30 x 30 um detector collects isotropic ion fluorescence, the turns at the two ends sit inside the collection footprint. Moving the turnarounds just outside the window edge (lines slightly longer than the Au opening) removes them from the optical region without changing the window.
